Nepal

A Hindu-majority republic with a deeply Buddhist valley at its heart, and a calendar, a clock and a set of gods that all run on their own logic.

Time & calendar

Nepal is 5 hours and 45 minutes ahead of UTC, and the year is 2083

The 45-minute offset is not an accident of colonial mapping — it is set to the meridian of Gaurishankar, a mountain east of Kathmandu, and Nepal keeps it partly to be distinct from India's half-hour offset. The official calendar is Bikram Sambat, which runs about 56 years and 8 months ahead of the Gregorian one: late 2026 is 2083 BS. Newspapers, government paperwork and most shop signs use it, so a date on a permit may not look like the date you expect.

Religion

The living goddess is a real child with a civil service around her

The Kumari is a pre-pubescent girl from the Newar Shakya caste, selected against a list of physical perfections and a demonstrated lack of fear, who is worshipped as the earthly manifestation of the goddess Taleju until she menstruates. She lives in the Kumari Ghar on Kathmandu Durbar Square, appears at a window for visitors, and is carried in procession at Indra Jatra. There are several Kumaris in the valley — Patan and Bhaktapur have their own — and former Kumaris receive a state pension.

Peoples

"Sherpa" is an ethnic group, not a job

The Sherpa are a Tibetan-descended people of the Solukhumbu, around 150,000 strong, with their own language and a Nyingma Buddhist tradition. Calling any porter a sherpa is a bit like calling every plumber a Fleming. On the Annapurna side you are far more likely to be walking through Gurung and Magar country — Gurung villages like Siding and Lwang, with their slate roofs and a strong tradition of service in the British and Indian Gurkha regiments.

Architecture

The valley's towns were three rival kingdoms

Kathmandu, Patan and Bhaktapur each had their own king, their own Durbar Square and their own court artists, and spent centuries competing in architecture. That is why three royal squares sit within eight miles of each other, and why each has a different character: Kathmandu's is the most built-over, Patan's the most refined, Bhaktapur's the best preserved. The unifying culture is Newar — the valley's indigenous people, whose craftsmen exported the pagoda form to Tibet and China.

Bhutan

A Vajrayana Buddhist kingdom that opened to tourism in 1974, measures its progress in happiness, and has never been colonised.

The name

Bhutan does not call itself Bhutan

The Bhutanese name is Druk Yul — the Land of the Thunder Dragon — and the people are Drukpa. The dragon on the flag clutches jewels, representing wealth, and its snarling mouth represents the strength of the deities protecting the country. "Bhutan" is an outside name, probably from Sanskrit Bhoṭa-anta, "the end of Tibet".

Government

The king gave away his own power, and had to insist

Bhutan became a constitutional democracy in 2008 not through revolution but because the fourth king, Jigme Singye Wangchuck, spent years pushing a reluctant population towards it, then abdicated in 2006 in favour of his son to make the transition stick. The same king coined Gross National Happiness in the 1970s. It is now a measured framework with nine domains and a national survey, used to screen policy — proposals are assessed for their happiness impact much as other countries assess environmental impact.

Architecture

Dzongs were built without nails, and without drawings

The great fortress-monasteries — Punakha, Paro Rinpung, Trongsa — are timber and rammed earth, assembled with interlocking joinery rather than nails, and traditionally built without architectural plans: a lama would determine the layout by inspiration and the master carpenter would work from it. Each dzong still houses both the district administration and the monk body, in separate courtyards, which is why you are visiting a government office and a temple in the same building.

Daily life

The national dress is a legal requirement, and has a pocket the size of a rucksack

Men wear the gho, a knee-length robe folded and belted to create an enormous chest pouch that historically carried a bowl, a knife and betel nut, and now carries a phone and paperwork. Women wear the kira, an ankle-length wrapped cloth. Both are required in dzongs, schools and government offices, along with a ceremonial scarf whose colour marks rank: white for ordinary citizens, saffron for the king and the Je Khenpo.

How to add one

No format needed. A voice note, a half-remembered fact, a photo of an information board, "the guide said something about why the bridges have those chains" — all fine. Each entry gets a category, a headline, a short write-up, and tags so related things can be found together.
