How to be a good guest

Tips, customs & things to experience

Two Himalayan Buddhist-and-Hindu cultures that look similar from Europe and are not remotely the same on the ground.

Things that are true in both countries

The body has a hierarchy

In Himalayan Buddhist and Hindu cultures alike, the head is the most sacred part of the body and the feet the least. Never pat anyone on the head, including children. Never point your soles at a person, an altar, a statue or a shrine — tuck your feet under you when you sit on a temple floor. Don't step over someone who's sitting or lying down. Step around, always.

Right hand, both hands

The left hand is the toilet hand. Eat, give, receive and point with the right. Better still, offer and accept anything of significance with both hands — money, a gift, a business card, a cup of tea. It's a small gesture that reads as respect everywhere from a Thimphu monastery to a Pokhara teashop.

Everything sacred is walked clockwise

Stupas, chortens, mani walls, prayer wheels, monasteries, the mountain itself. Keep the sacred object on your right and spin the prayer wheels clockwise as you pass. At Boudhanath you'll simply be carried along by the crowd; at a mani wall on the Mardi ridge you'll be the only one doing it, and you should still do it.

Shoes and hats off, voices down

Remove shoes and headwear before entering any temple, monastery, dzong or private home. Cover shoulders and knees. Don't touch statues, offerings, altars or religious texts, and never turn your back on an altar to take a photo. Photography is usually forbidden inside shrines in both countries and freely allowed outside — ask, and accept the first answer.

Ask before you photograph a person

Especially monks, nuns, elderly people and anyone at prayer. A raised eyebrow and a lifted camera is enough of a question. At Pashupatinath's cremation ghats the answer is no and you shouldn't be asking — those are real families on the worst day of their lives.

Public affection, and dress

Holding hands is fine. Kissing in public is not — you'll see men holding hands as friends far more often than couples doing anything. Dress modestly away from the tourist strips: shoulders and knees covered, no vests for men in towns or temples. Nobody will say anything; they'll just think less of you.

Nepal

Etiquette, the Nepali specifics

  • Namaste, palms together at the chest, head slightly bowed. Use it constantly. Namaskar is the more respectful version for elders. It works as hello, goodbye, thank you and sorry-I-don't-speak-Nepali.
  • The head wobble is a yes. That side-to-side tilt means agreement, or "fine", or "I'm listening". It is not ambivalence.
  • "Jutho" — ritual pollution by saliva. Once food or drink has touched your lips it's contaminated for everyone else. Don't offer someone a bite of your momo or a sip of your bottle, don't drink straight from a shared jug (Nepalis pour into the mouth without touching), and don't take food from a communal dish with the spoon you've been eating with.
  • Non-Hindus may not enter the main Pashupatinath temple or certain inner sanctums elsewhere. The surrounding complex, the ghats and the terraces are completely open, and that's where everything happens anyway. Don't argue at the gate.
  • Leather — belts, bags, jackets — is banned inside some Hindu temples. Pashupatinath's guards will point at your belt if it's an issue.
  • Women during menstruation are traditionally excluded from some temples and kitchens. Nobody will ask, but the custom exists and is taken seriously in rural households.
  • Don't hand money or gifts to children. It funds a begging economy that keeps them out of school. If you want to give, give to a school or a registered NGO.
  • Beef. The cow is sacred and killing one is a criminal offence. You'll see buff (water buffalo) on menus instead — it's excellent, especially in momos.
  • Tipping: 10% in restaurants where service isn't included, NPR 100–200 for a hotel porter, and round up taxi fares. On the trek, see the Practical page — that one matters.

What to eat, and where

The essential list

  • Dal bhat tarkari — lentil soup, rice, seasonal vegetable curry, pickle, sometimes meat or curd. The national dish, eaten twice a day by most Nepalis, and refills are free and expected. On the trail it's the only thing cooked fresh all day. "Dal bhat power, 24 hour," as every porter will tell you.
  • Momos — steamed or fried dumplings, buff or chicken or veg, with a tomato-sesame achar. Also jhol momo, swimming in a spiced broth, which is better.
  • Newari food, the valley's own cuisine and the reason Patan and Bhaktapur are worth eating in: bara (lentil pancake with egg), chatamari ("Newari pizza" on a rice crêpe), choila (fiercely spiced grilled buffalo), yomari (sweet steamed dumpling), samay baji (a ceremonial platter of beaten rice, black soybean, ginger, egg and choila — order it, share it, it's the whole cuisine on one plate).
  • Thakali khana set — the refined northern version of dal bhat, from the Thak Khola near Mustang. If you see a Thakali kitchen, go in.
  • Sel roti, a sweet ring of fried rice dough — everywhere during Dashain.
  • Juju dhau — Bhaktapur's "king of curds", set in a clay pot. Non-negotiable on the morning of the 15th.
  • Chiya, milk tea with cardamom and ginger. And tongba, warm fermented millet drunk through a bamboo straw with hot water topped up — a Himalayan evening in a pot.
  • Raksi, the local spirit. Everest and Gorkha beer. Nepali whisky is best avoided.

Where

  • Patan: Honacha behind the Durbar Square for the unpolished Newari original — you sit on a low bench and point. Café Swotha, Yala Mandala and Newa Chen for the same food with a courtyard and a wine list. Dhokaima Café for a garden lunch.
  • Boudhanath: any rooftop facing the stupa. The Tibetan bakeries around the kora do good momos and better coffee.
  • Thamel: touristy but genuinely good — OR2K, Or Bar, Fire & Ice for pizza when you've had enough dal bhat, and the Rum Doodle for the mountaineering history on the walls.
  • Bhaktapur: the Peacock Guest House terrace on Dattatreya Square, late afternoon.
  • Pokhara Lakeside: bakeries for the post-trek breakfast you've been dreaming about at 3,500 m. Try a Thakali set at one of the local kitchens off the main strip.
  • Vegetarians eat extremely well in both countries; vegans should learn to say "no ghee, no curd" and check on dal.

Ten things worth going out of your way for

  1. Boudhanath at 06:30. Not at noon. The kora with pilgrims, butter lamps, the smell of juniper smoke, monks in maroon doing prostrations. The best single hour in Kathmandu.
  2. The evening aarti at Pashupatinath. Fire, bells, chanting, the river, the ghats. Sit on the east-bank terraces from about 17:45.
  3. The Patan Museum. Genuinely world-class — a small, beautifully curated collection of Newari bronzes in the old royal palace, with the labelling of a European national museum.
  4. Bhaktapur before eight in the morning. Empty medieval squares, potters at work, the smell of wood smoke. The tour buses ruin it by ten.
  5. Sunrise from Sarangkot. Which you have booked for the 7th.
  6. The first sight of Machhapuchhre from Low Camp. No photograph prepares you for how close and how vertical it is.
  7. A rowboat on Phewa Lake at dusk, out to Tal Barahi and back, with the Annapurnas reflected if the air is still.
  8. Paragliding off Sarangkot. Top-three site in the world, thermal-rich, and you land by the lake.
  9. A cooking class in Kathmandu — half a day, market trip included, and momos become a thing you can do at home.
  10. Dashain itself. Bamboo swings at every crossroads, kites all afternoon, marigold garlands, families in new clothes, the entire country moving. You'll catch the first week of it.

Buying things well. Pashmina: real cashmere is soft, slightly uneven, and burns to ash smelling of hair — acrylic melts into a bead and smells of plastic; vendors will do the burn test for you. Thangkas: ask who painted it and how long it took, and expect to pay properly for a hand-painted one. Singing bowls: hand-hammered bowls have visible marks and a long complex ring; machine-cast ones are smooth and thin. In Thamel start at 40–50% of the asking price and settle around 65–75%; do it with a smile, it's a conversation, not a fight. If haggling isn't your thing, Mahaguthi, Dhukuti and the fair-trade shops in Patan and Kupondole are fixed-price, good quality and the money goes to the artisans.

On the trail — teahouse and trekking etiquette

The unwritten rules

  • Eat where you sleep. Teahouse rooms are cheap or free because the lodge makes its money on food. Ordering dinner elsewhere is a real discourtesy.
  • Order dinner when you arrive, not at seven. One stove, one cook, twenty trekkers.
  • Uphill has right of way, and always step to the inside of the trail — the mountain side, never the drop side — when mules or a dzo train passes. Animals don't brake and they don't care.
  • Greet everyone. Namaste to every porter, every trekker, every grandmother carrying sixty kilos of firewood. It costs nothing and it's how the trail works.
  • Don't photograph porters as scenery. Talk to them first.
  • Carry your rubbish down, including fruit peel and tissue. Above Kande there is no waste collection worth the name.
  • No plastic bottles. Refill and treat. It's the single biggest thing a trekker can do for the Annapurna region.
  • Rooms are plywood boxes. Whisper after nine, and use a head torch with a red mode.

Practical trail realities

  • Toilets range from Western-style at Forest Camp to a squat over a hole at High Camp. Carry your own paper and hand gel; put paper in the bin, not the pan.
  • Hot showers where they exist are solar-heated buckets and cost NPR 300–500. There is no shower at High Camp. Wet wipes are your friend.
  • It gets dark at 18:00 and the dining hut is the only heated room. Bring a book, cards, and the down jacket to wear indoors.
  • Blankets are thin. A liner or your own bag makes the difference between sleeping and enduring.
  • Boil your morning slowly. Breakfast at 3,500 m takes forever to arrive; order it the night before.
  • Poles. The descent to Siding on day five is 1,400 m of steep knee-destroying zig-zag. Rent or buy a pair in Pokhara for a few euros.
  • Leeches are a monsoon problem and October is past it — but after rain in the low forest below Forest Camp you may still meet one. Salt or a fingernail; they're harmless.

Things to be mildly wary of

  • The tika scam. A friendly "holy man" at Pashupatinath or Kathmandu Durbar Square marks your forehead, then demands a large donation. A firm "no thank you" before he reaches you, and keep walking.
  • Unlicensed trekking guides offering a cheap deal in Lakeside. Beyond the legal problem, they carry no insurance and no liability. Book through a registered agency.
  • Taxi meters. Nobody uses them. Agree the price first or use Pathao / InDrive.
  • ATM skimming does happen. Use machines inside bank branches, cover the keypad, and check statements.
  • Monkeys at Swayambhunath and Pashupatinath will take sunglasses, food and phones. Don't carry anything loose, don't make eye contact, don't smile at them — bared teeth is a threat.
  • Kathmandu's air in the dry season is genuinely bad. An FFP2 mask in traffic is not paranoid.
  • Strikes (bandhs) can shut roads at no notice, mostly around politics. Your guide or hotel will know; build in the airport buffer.
  • Altitude is the real risk on this trip, not crime. Nepal is a strikingly safe country for visitors.

Bhutan

Driglam Namzha — the code of conduct

Bhutan has a formal, codified etiquette called Driglam Namzha that governs dress, posture, speech and deference. Nobody expects a visitor to know it, but visibly trying earns you a lot, and your guide will steer you. The essentials:

  • Dress properly for dzongs. Long trousers or a long skirt, sleeves, collar if you have one. No shorts, no vests, no hats inside. Dzongs are working government offices as well as monasteries — you are visiting a courthouse and a temple at once. Bhutanese law requires citizens to wear the gho and kira there, with the ceremonial kabney scarf; dressing scruffily as a foreigner reads as contempt.
  • You're wearing national dress on 30 September at Punakha Dzong. Take it seriously and enjoy it — it's a compliment to your hosts, and you will look better in a gho and kira than you expect.
  • Remove shoes and hats before entering any temple. Leave cameras and bags outside or in lockers where asked.
  • Don't point with a finger — use an open hand, palm up. Don't point your feet at an altar or a person.
  • Don't touch a monk, or anyone's head. Don't shake a monk's hand unless he offers first.
  • Speak quietly in religious spaces, and don't sit with your back to a statue.
  • Accept what's offered. Tea and snacks in a home should be accepted, even a token sip. Traditionally you decline twice and accept on the third offer — your hosts will not hold you to it.
  • The royal family is genuinely, uncomplicatedly loved. Their portraits are everywhere. Don't be clever about it.
  • Photography: ask before photographing people, and assume no inside temples. Tiger's Nest interiors are strictly off-limits — leave the camera in the cafeteria lockers.

What to eat

The essentials

  • Ema datshi — chillies stewed in yak or cow cheese. This is not a dish with chilli in it; the chilli is the vegetable. It's the national dish and it is seriously hot. Variations: kewa datshi (potato), shamu datshi (mushroom), both much gentler entry points.
  • Red rice — nutty, slightly sticky, grown in the Paro and Punakha valleys. Served with everything.
  • Suja — butter tea, churned with salt and yak butter. Closer to a thin soup than to tea. Try it at least once, ideally somewhere cold.
  • Ara — the home-distilled spirit of rice, maize or millet, sometimes served warm with a fried egg in it. Farmhouse hospitality in a cup.
  • Momos here too, and hoentay (buckwheat dumplings from Haa), puta (buckwheat noodles from Bumthang), jasha maru (spicy minced chicken), phaksha paa (pork belly with radish and dried chilli).
  • Zaow, roasted rice, offered with tea in rural homes — this is what the wooden cup Breathe Bhutan gives you is for. Carry it.
  • Local beers: Red Panda (an unfiltered wheat beer from Bumthang, very good) and Druk 11000. The Namgay Artisanal Brewery in Paro on your last evening is worth the stop.

Eating well on your package

  • Breathe Bhutan deliberately leaves your restaurants open rather than pre-booking them. Use that. Tell your guide on day one that you want to eat Bhutanese, not buffet — hotel buffets in Bhutan are built for tour groups and are dull.
  • Ask for "ezay" — the fresh chilli-and-cheese relish on the side. And say "not too spicy" if you mean it; Bhutanese "medium" is European "help".
  • The homecooked meal with a local family and the picnic by the river in your package are the two best meals of the nine days. Don't fill up beforehand.
  • Vegetarian is easy — Buddhist Bhutan eats a lot of vegetables and dairy. Vegan is harder; the cheese is in everything.

Things to ask your guide for

Your itinerary is already dense, but Bhutanese guides are flexible and these slot in easily:

  • Archery. The national sport, and nothing like the Olympic version: targets 145 m apart, teammates standing beside the target heckling, singing and dancing when someone scores. There's a match on most weekends at the Changlimithang ground in Thimphu. Ask to watch, and ask to try — traditional bamboo bows are lethal and hilarious.
  • A local tsechu. Masked dance festivals happen valley by valley through autumn on the lunar calendar. Ask your guide whether one falls in your window — even a small village tsechu beats any museum.
  • Morning prayers at a monastery. You have this on 1 October at Gangtey Shedra; if you love it, ask for another.
  • The hot stone bath, outdoors. Breathe Bhutan specifically recommends the version by a stream rather than in a hotel. Take that option.
  • Doma. Betel nut with lime wrapped in a leaf, chewed by half the country, which is why the smiles are red. Try it once, spit it out, don't swallow.
  • The phalluses. Painted on house walls across the country to ward off evil, courtesy of Drukpa Kunley, the 15th-century "Divine Madman" whose temple you visit at Chimi Lhakhang on 3 October. It is not a joke at your expense; it's a genuine and cheerful folk tradition.
  • Ask about Gross National Happiness — but ask properly. It's a real, operationalised policy framework with nine domains and a survey instrument, not a marketing line, and educated Bhutanese have complicated, interesting views on how well it works.
  • Buy: handwoven yathra wool from Bumthang, kira and gho fabric, handmade desho paper, incense, a wooden dapa bowl. Antiques cannot be exported without a clearance certificate, and religious items over 100 years old are flatly prohibited — buy new.

Rules that will surprise you

  • Tobacco is heavily restricted. Selling it is illegal, smoking in public places is banned and fined, and importing it carries steep duty. If either of you smokes, declare it at Paro customs and pay the tax — don't chance it.
  • Tuesday is a dry day. No alcohol sold nationally. Bars are closed. Your hotel may quietly serve you; don't make it a project.
  • You cannot travel independently. Every day of your stay is with a licensed guide — that's the model, and it's the reason the country feels the way it does.
  • The Sustainable Development Fee of US$100 per person per night is already in your package (US$1,600 of it). It funds free healthcare, free education and carbon-negative forestry. It's not a tourist tax in the usual sense.
  • Bhutan is the only carbon-negative country on earth. 60%+ forest cover is written into the constitution. Littering is genuinely shocking to people.
  • No traffic lights in the whole country. Thimphu has a white-gloved policeman in a pavilion instead; they installed a light once and took it out because people hated it.
  • Television and internet arrived in 1999. Within living memory.
  • Mountaineering above 6,000 m is banned — the peaks are the homes of deities. Gangkhar Puensum, at 7,570 m, is the highest unclimbed mountain in the world and will stay that way.
  • Plastic bags have been banned since 1999, and single-use plastics are discouraged everywhere. Bring a bottle.
  • Bhutan runs 15 minutes ahead of Nepal (UTC+6 vs UTC+5:45), and half an hour ahead of India.

For the Bumdra nights (4–5 October). The camp sits at about 3,800 m, higher than anywhere you sleep on Mardi Himal. Expect around freezing overnight. Carry a head torch, a warm hat, thermals and gloves in your daypack, not in the duffel on the horse. And on the 5th, remember: no bags, phones or cameras inside Tiger's Nest — lockers are at the cafeteria, so take your photographs on the way in.

One last thing. The two countries will feel very different, and it's worth naming why. Nepal is loud, chaotic, poor, extraordinarily warm, and completely open to you — you can go where you like and nobody is managing your experience. Bhutan is quiet, ordered, carefully curated, and you will see it through a guide. Neither is more "authentic". Take Nepal's chaos as generosity and Bhutan's control as care, and you'll get the best of both.
