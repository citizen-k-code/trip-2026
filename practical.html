Do these first

You are in the air today and the Nepal leg is unbooked. None of it is hard, but two items are time-critical.

① The trekking guide — start this from the Bangkok layover or your first evening in Kathmandu. A licensed guide is legally required on Mardi Himal, and he has to come through a government-registered agency. Good agencies can put a trek together in 48 hours, but you want it agreed before you disappear into Bhutan on the 28th, because Bhutanese hotel wifi and a packed touring schedule make admin miserable.

② The two domestic flights (6 and 14 October). The 14 October Pokhara → Kathmandu flight falls in Dashain week, the busiest travel period of the Nepali year. Seats sell out and prices climb. Book both legs now, together.

The trek: guide, permits, money

A guide is not optional

Since 1 April 2023 foreign trekkers cannot legally walk Nepal's national-park and conservation-area trails independently. Mardi Himal is inside the Annapurna Conservation Area, so it applies to you. The guide must be licensed and engaged through a government-registered trekking agency — a freelancer you meet in Lakeside does not satisfy the rule, and checkpoints do ask.

Practically this is a good thing here: your guide handles the permits, books teahouse rooms ahead (High Camp fills in October), reads the weather on summit morning and knows which lodges have the decent food.

Choosing an agency

Three sensible routes, in order of least effort:

  • Ask Breathe Bhutan for a referral. Good Himalayan operators know their counterparts across the border and their reputation travels with the recommendation. Email them today — they're already your most invested contact.
  • Ask Tajaa Pha. Heritage hotels in Patan deal with this weekly and will have a trusted agency.
  • Book direct with a Pokhara-based agency. Pokhara operators are 15–25% cheaper than Kathmandu ones for Annapurna treks because there's no transfer leg in the price.

Check before paying: registered with the Nepal Ministry of Tourism and a member of TAAN (Trekking Agencies' Association of Nepal); the guide's licence number; that the guide and porter are insured; and get the itinerary and inclusions in writing by email. Pay a deposit, the balance in Pokhara.

Booking the two Nepali domestic legs

  • Buddha Air (buddhaair.com) is the safest bet: 17+ daily Kathmandu–Pokhara rotations, ATR turboprops, the best operational record among Nepali domestics. Yeti Airlines is the usual alternative and often US$10–20 cheaper.
  • Foreigners pay a higher published fare than Nepalis — reckon US$90–125 one way. Book direct on the airline site with a card, or let your Kathmandu hotel or the trekking agency issue the tickets (they charge a small fee and it saves card-decline headaches).
  • Baggage: 20 kg checked + 5 kg hand per person, excess around NPR 100–150/kg paid at check-in. Your Thai bags are 23 kg, so expect a small excess charge — not a problem, just carry rupees.
  • Flights land at Pokhara International Airport (PKR), the newer field east of the lake, about 8–10 km and 45 minutes from Sarangkot.
  • Weather: morning flights are the reliable ones; cancellations happen and airlines rebook you onto the next available service. That is exactly why 13 October is a buffer day.

Connecting at Kathmandu on 6 October

  1. 08:10 — arrive from Paro

    International terminal. Immigration re-entry on your existing multiple-entry Nepali visa, then baggage. Allow 60–90 minutes.

  2. ~09:40 — cross to domestic

    About 500 m. Walk it in 5–10 minutes or take the free shuttle (every ~20 min). You must exit and re-enter through security — there is no airside connection.

  3. Check in separately

    Domestic check-in closes 45–60 minutes before departure. With an 08:10 landing, a departure from 12:00 onwards is comfortable; 11:00 is possible but leaves nothing for a late Drukair arrival.

If you would rather not gamble: a 14:00–15:00 Pokhara flight still gets you to Sarangkot in daylight for sunset.

11–13 Oct · Pokhara Lakeside

Three nights, after five in teahouses. Prioritise: strong hot water, a garden or pool, and the north end of Lakeside (quieter, better views, short walk to the strip).

Candidates to check: Temple Tree Resort & Spa, Hotel Barahi, Fish Tail Lodge (own peninsula), Mount Kailash Resort, Waterfront Resort. Roughly €45–110 a night for a good double.

14 Oct · Bhaktapur

Stay inside the walled old city — that is the entire point. Small heritage guesthouses on or near Durbar, Taumadhi and Pottery squares.

Candidates: Hotel Heritage, Peacock Guest House (on Dattatreya Square, the loveliest restaurant terrace in the valley), Milla Guest House, Bhaktapur Paradise. €25–70.

Note the Bhaktapur entry ticket (~NPR 1,800 pp) — hotel guests can usually get it validated for the length of their stay, so ask.

15–16 Oct · Kathmandu or Patan

Patan is the calmer, better-fed choice and you'll already know it. Thamel is louder but puts the shopping on your doorstep.

Patan: Inn Patan, Cosy Nepal's restored houses, or Tajaa Pha again. Kathmandu: Kantipur Temple House, Hotel Shanker, Traditional Comfort; Dwarika's if you want to end the trip with a bang (€250+). Boudha: Hyatt Regency or a small stupa-side guesthouse if you want to wake up to the kora.

Dashain caveat. From 11 October hotel demand in Pokhara and Kathmandu rises sharply and some family-run places close for the main festival days. Book with free cancellation where you can, but book now rather than on arrival.

Landing in Kathmandu, step by step

  1. Before you land — fill the arrival form online

    Nepal's Department of Immigration lets you complete the tourist visa application in advance at online.nepalimmigration.gov.np. The submission is valid for 15 days, so do it during the Bangkok layover, not before you leave Belgium. Print or screenshot the barcode receipt. It turns the kiosk queue into a scan.

  2. Kiosks, or the form by hand

    If you didn't pre-fill, use the touchscreen kiosks in the arrivals hall — they print the slip you take to the payment desk. Half of them are usually broken; the paper forms at the side are faster when the hall is busy.

  3. Pay the visa fee — cash

    US$50 each for 30 days (15 days is $30, 90 days $125). You need 22 days, so take the 30. Pay in cash: USD is cleanest, EUR is accepted at a poor rate, and card machines are unreliable. Bring US$100 in clean, unfolded notes for the two of you and don't expect change. Belgian passports are eligible for visa on arrival.

  4. Immigration

    Hand over passport, payment receipt and a passport photo (they usually take a webcam photo instead, but carry two spares each). This is the slow queue — 30 to 60 minutes is normal off a wide-body.

  5. Your visa is multiple-entry

    Nepali tourist visas allow multiple entries as standard, so the Bhutan trip does not need anything extra. But the clock runs on calendar days from first entry, including the days you're in Bhutan: 26 September to 17 October is 22 days of a 30-day visa. Comfortable.

  6. Bags, then SIM, then money

    Ncell and Nepal Telecom both have counters in the arrivals hall; the ATMs are just past customs. Do both before you leave the building.

  7. Out to the taxi

    Prepaid taxi desk inside, or walk past the touts to the ranks. To Patan: NPR 1,200–1,800, 35–50 minutes. Agree the fare before getting in. Pathao and InDrive both work in Kathmandu and are cheaper if you've got data already.

Money

Nepal

  • Nepali rupee (NPR). Roughly NPR 155–165 to €1 and NPR 135–140 to US$1 in late 2026 — check on the day; the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee, so it moves with the INR.
  • ATMs: a flat fee of about NPR 500 per withdrawal and a cap of NPR 35,000 on most machines (Nabil, Himalayan, Standard Chartered, NIC Asia are the reliable ones). So withdraw the maximum each time, not little and often. Tell your bank you're travelling.
  • Cash to carry: US$100 for the two visas, plus €200–300 in cash as a reserve. Exchange counters in Thamel and Lakeside beat the banks.
  • On the trek there are no ATMs and no cards. Draw everything in Pokhara before you go up: budget NPR 45,000–60,000 for the two of you for five days of teahouses, drinks, showers, charging and tips. Bring small notes — nobody on the ridge can change a 1,000.
  • Cards work in mid-range hotels, Lakeside and Thamel restaurants and bigger shops, usually with a 3–4% surcharge. Everywhere else is cash.
  • You cannot convert rupees back outside Nepal. Spend or change them before you fly, and keep your exchange receipts if you want to reconvert at the airport.

Bhutan

  • Your package covers essentially everything — hotels, all three meals, vehicle, guide, driver, entries, SDF. You need cash only for tips, drinks, shopping and the odd temple offering.
  • Ngultrum (BTN), pegged 1:1 to the Indian rupee. Indian rupees circulate freely, but not INR 500 notes — only 100s and below.
  • Best exchange is at Paro airport on arrival, or Bank of Bhutan / BNB in Thimphu. ATMs exist in Thimphu, Paro and Punakha and take Visa/Mastercard, but treat them as a backup — empty machines are common.
  • Bring US$400–600 in cash for the nine days. That comfortably covers tips plus a decent textile purchase.
  • Tipping: not required, but now expected in tourism. Roughly US$10–20 per day for the guide and US$10 per day for the driver, from the two of you together, given in an envelope on the last evening. For nine days that's about US$180–270 total. Keep it separate in your bag from day one.
  • Not covered by your package: alcohol, meals at luxury hotels you're not staying in, shopping, tips, horse riding at Tiger's Nest.

Phone and data

Nepal

  • Both Ncell and Nepal Telecom (NTC) have counters in the Kathmandu arrivals hall, both now sell eSIMs as well as physical SIMs, and both will set it up for you in 5–10 minutes. Passport required — they photograph it. Pay in rupees or dollars, cash.
  • Ncell is faster and more reliable in Kathmandu and Pokhara; their tourist packs run roughly NPR 500–1,500 for 5–15 GB over 7–30 days. Take the 30-day option.
  • NTC generally holds signal better in the mountains. If one of you takes Ncell and the other NTC, you have coverage almost everywhere on the Mardi ridge — there is patchy but real signal at Low Camp and High Camp.
  • Teahouse wifi exists above Forest Camp and costs NPR 200–500 a night. It is slow. Treat the trek as five days offline and tell people at home in advance.
  • Charging on the trail costs NPR 200–400 per device per charge, from a solar bank. Bring a 20,000 mAh power bank and you can skip it entirely — cold drains phone batteries fast, so sleep with them in your bag.

Bhutan

  • Tourist SIMs from B-Mobile (Bhutan Telecom) or TashiCell are sold at Paro airport and in Thimphu for a few hundred ngultrum — your guide will sort it in five minutes if you ask on day one.
  • Honestly, you may not need one. Hotels have wifi, you are with a guide who has a phone all day, and nine days of lighter connectivity is not a hardship in Bhutan of all places.
  • Bhutan is UTC+6, Nepal is UTC+5:45. Your phone will handle the 15-minute jump; your body clock won't notice.
  • Power: Bhutan uses 230 V with type D/F/G sockets, Nepal 230 V with type C/D/M. One universal adapter each covers both — and bring a short multi-socket so a single adapter charges four things at a teahouse.

Insurance, health, altitude

Insurance — check this today

Most standard European travel policies exclude trekking above 3,000 or 4,000 m, and almost all exclude helicopter evacuation unless you've bought the adventure add-on. You need:

  • Trekking cover to 4,500 m (non-technical, no ropes) — say "trekking, not mountaineering"
  • Helicopter search & rescue and evacuation — the only way off the Mardi ridge in an emergency, and a heli-evac from High Camp runs US$3,000–6,000
  • Medical repatriation and cover for Bhutan as well as Nepal

Belgian options that cover high-altitude trekking include Allianz Global Assistance and Europ Assistance add-ons, or a specialist policy from Globalis / World Nomads. Carry the policy number and the 24/7 emergency line on paper — your phone may be dead or out of signal when you need it.

Altitude

  • Your profile is good. Bumdra at 3,800 m on 4 October, then Mardi's High Camp at 3,580 m on 9 October: you arrive pre-acclimatised, which is the single biggest safety factor.
  • Watch for: headache that won't shift, nausea, dizziness, breathlessness at rest, poor sleep and loss of appetite. Mild symptoms at 3,500 m are normal; worsening symptoms mean going down, not pushing on.
  • Acetazolamide (Diamox) 125 mg twice daily is worth carrying as a prophylactic for the High Camp night — ask your GP before leaving, or buy it over the counter in any Kathmandu pharmacy for a couple of euros.
  • Drink 3–4 litres a day, eat more than you feel like, skip the alcohol above Low Camp, and never sleep higher than you need to. Going down 370 m to Badal Danda on the 10th is the plan for exactly this reason.

Stomach and water

  • Never drink the tap. Bottled water is fine in cities; on the trail, refill from the teahouse boiled-water service (NPR 100–200/l) or treat it yourself with a SteriPen, a Sawyer filter or chlorine dioxide tablets. Buying plastic bottles up the ridge is both expensive and locally frowned on.
  • Cooked, hot and fresh is the rule. Dal bhat is made fresh all day and is the safest thing on any teahouse menu — and refills are free.
  • Carry Imodium, oral rehydration salts and a course of azithromycin (ask your GP). Traveller's diarrhoea on day 3 of a trek is the classic trip-ruiner and the classic easily-treated thing.
  • Hand gel, and use it before every meal.

Before you go / on arrival

  • Vaccinations: hepatitis A, typhoid, tetanus-diphtheria-polio as standard; hepatitis B and rabies if you've time. No yellow-fever requirement coming from Belgium. Nothing is required for entry.
  • Malaria: not a concern at any altitude you'll be at — Kathmandu, Pokhara, Bhutan's western valleys are all above the risk zone.
  • If something goes wrong in Kathmandu: CIWEC Clinic (Lainchaur) is the expat-standard travel-medicine clinic in Nepal, with altitude specialists; Nepal Mediciti and Grande International are the big private hospitals. In Pokhara, CIWEC has a branch in Lakeside.
  • Sun: at 4,200 m the UV is brutal even when it's cold. Factor 50, lip balm with SPF, and proper category-3 or -4 sunglasses.
